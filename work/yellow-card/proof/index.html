A teardown for Yellow Card

How Yellow Card Positions Its Dollar Account to Treasurers and Chief Financial Officers

James Praise · Marketing and Go-to-Market Operator

Built from Yellow Card's public pages, read on 4 November 2026, and the Growth Marketing Lead posting. I have no access to Yellow Card's ad accounts, Salesforce or sales calls, so the findings describe what a treasurer sees from outside, and the 90 day plan is a set of hypotheses to test, not a diagnosis. A proposal, not affiliated with Yellow Card.

The problem the pivot creates

Yellow Card closed its consumer app on 1 January 2026 and now sells stablecoin and fiat infrastructure to businesses (Techsoma). The buyer for the business dollar account is a treasurer or chief financial officer at a multinational, with a long cycle and several people on the buying committee. That buyer is not a retail user, a bank or a payment company, and the pages built for those earlier audiences do not address them.

Yellow Card has a real product, fresh money and four named customers. The pages a treasurer lands on do not yet make that buyer feel addressed, give them one number they can check, or answer the first question a compliance reviewer asks.

I found seven gaps. I think three of them, the buyer not being addressed, the number that cannot be checked and the unanswered compliance question, cost more pipeline than any channel choice, and work on all three can start before paid spend scales.

What they say

The company. The homepage headline is "The Infrastructure for Modern Money Movement," with the subhead "Digital Asset & Fiat Infrastructure for Emerging Markets." The intro line addresses "banks, financial institutions, corporates, and other businesses." The stats are $10B+ processed, 106+ Tier 1 banking and liquidity partners, 60+ countries and 50+ payment currencies. Visa is the only customer named on the page.

The dollar account. The product page is called Global Accounts. Its headline is "Global accounts. Built for business across borders." It offers dedicated account details in the business's name, including US dollar and euro accounts, conversion between local currencies and stablecoins, and payments to suppliers. It then splits into two audiences: "Your treasury, without borders" and "Your platform. Their global account" for companies that embed accounts in their own product. The launch post adds pound sterling accounts, connections to the international Automated Clearing House (ACH) network, the SWIFT interbank messaging network and Fedwire (the United States Federal Reserve payment system), and one application programming interface (API) or treasury portal.

The funding story. The $40 million release describes the product as a "single account to hold U.S. dollars, hold and swap stablecoins, manage treasury." It says Visa and Western Union already use it, and that Yellow Card holds licences, authorisations and registrations in 22 jurisdictions.

The treasurer pitch. One article speaks to the buyer in the job posting. "Stop Paying an 8% Tax to Access Your Own Profits," bylined to the Chief Operating Officer Maria Oldham on 1 July 2026, is written for corporate treasurers at multinationals with subsidiaries in emerging markets. It claims that moving profit home through correspondent banking costs 8.3 percent and takes 42 days, against about 0.8 percent and 24 hours through Yellow Card. A second article, from 27 October 2026, argues that a business should control when it converts currency, and pairs control with visibility.

Who the buyer is

The posting names treasurers, treasury directors and chief financial officers at large multinationals, with long cycles, several people on each buying committee, and named accounts in commodities, logistics, mining, fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG), pharmaceutical and manufacturing.

My read of that committee is an inference, not Yellow Card data. The treasurer feels the pain of trapped cash and currency swings, the chief financial officer signs, and a compliance or risk reviewer, a banking relationship owner and an information security reviewer can each stop the deal. A treasurer needs to explain the product to all of them, so the pages have to work as material the treasurer forwards.

What the site addresses is wider. The homepage speaks to banks, financial institutions and corporates. The four case studies are Western Union (27 October 2026), Thunes (14 October), Visa (23 September) and Xoom (23 September). The Western Union piece ends by addressing banks, telcos and payment service providers. The Visa piece closes on "any large financial institution" and names no treasurer. None of the four carries a metric.

The gaps

Gap 1: The proof is for a different buyer

Western Union, Thunes, Visa and Xoom are payment companies. A treasurer at a mining or pharmaceutical group does not see a peer. The only content aimed at corporate treasurers is the single 8 percent article. I found no page in the English sitemap for mining, FMCG or pharmaceutical buyers. The two sector articles that exist, on manufacturing margins and logistics payment delays, are dated 29 May 2025 and 28 May 2025, before the pivot to business customers. They are written to African manufacturers and freight companies, with calls to action such as "Stop absorbing FX losses and start trading smarter." That tone suits a regional business owner, and a multinational treasurer will read it as retail.

Gap 2: The headline number cannot be checked, and its arithmetic does not close

The 8.3 percent and 42 day figures in the 8 percent article have no source. The worked example says a $4.2 million profit arrives as $3.85 million and that "347,500" was lost. Three numbers describe the same loss.

CalculationResult
$4,200,000 minus $3,850,000$350,000
8.3 percent of $4,200,000$348,600
The loss the article states$347,500

A chief financial officer who tests the example and finds it off by a few thousand dollars discounts the rest of the claim. The article is also the strongest asset Yellow Card has for this buyer, so the fix is worth doing first.

Gap 3: Footprint numbers disagree across pages

Each figure below may be correct, because countries supported, countries reached and licensed jurisdictions measure different things. The pages do not say so, and a compliance reviewer compares them side by side.

WhereWhat it says
Homepage60+ countries supported
Launch post190+ countries reached, and "9 active licenses and registrations"
Funding releaseLicences in 22 jurisdictions
LinkedInA summary of recent posts describes operating in 34 countries
About pageA team across 20 countries

Gap 4: The first diligence question has no visible answer

A treasurer asks who holds the dollars. The launch post says "established banking partners" and names none. Yellow Card's legal section hosts an Erebor commercial deposit account agreement, a funds availability disclosure and a stablecoin custodial agreement, so a partner bank appears to exist, but I could not read what the pages say and cannot confirm its role. The Global Accounts page does well on the adjacent question, with International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 27001, System and Organization Controls (SOC) 1 and SOC 2 documentation and a Trust Center link. The partner bank, how funds are protected and which licence covers which corridor are the items that remain.

Gap 5: One page tries to serve two buyers

The Global Accounts page splits into "your treasury" and "their global account" for companies embedding accounts in their products. A treasurer clicking from a LinkedIn ad lands on a page that asks them to choose. The treasury version exists as a separate Treasury page, whose headline is "Fast, cost-effective treasury services in emerging markets" and which names no audience. When I fetched it as text, the counters displayed "$0B+" and "0+," which may be animated numbers that did not render for me. That is worth a browser check.

Gap 6: Three names for one product

The funding release and launch post say Global USD Accounts. The navigation and product page say Global Accounts. The posting says dollar account. The Western Union case study does not use the phrase Global USD Account at all. Search, ad copy and sales emails need one name.

Gap 7: Every path ends in the same ask

Across the pages I read, the calls to action are "Speak to an expert," "Book a call today" and "Book A Demo." A treasurer who is not ready to talk to sales has no smaller next step, such as a worksheet, a corridor cost estimate or a diligence pack.

A smaller note on freshness. Every blog page I fetched reports a publish timestamp of 28 October 2026 in its metadata, while the visible bylines run from May 2025 to October 2026. This may be a content system migration. Organic and AI search, which the posting calls an active channel, reads dates, so it is worth confirming.

What I would do in the first 90 days

If I was to take this role, I would start by finding out what the pipeline numbers already say, then fix the pages every channel sends people to, and only then spend.

Days 1 to 30: definitions, baseline and the account list

I would pull the last year of opportunities from Salesforce by source and stage, and write down what counts as a qualified lead, a marketing qualified lead (MQL), a sales accepted lead and a sourced opportunity, agreed with sales and marketing operations. Without this, a cost per MQL tells nobody anything. I would set a campaign naming and tracking standard, so spend connects to pipeline at every stage and a weak channel can be traced to the stage that broke. At FunnelEnvy I built the cross-brand measurement stack on Google Analytics 4, Looker Studio, HubSpot and Lightdash. At Marketing In Action (MIA) I reconcile what the ad platforms report against real sales, which is how I found Meta cross-crediting sales from other products.

With sales, I would build the named list and tier it by revenue exposure in the emerging markets Yellow Card serves, number of subsidiaries and whether a treasury owner can be found. I would start with the logistics and manufacturing accounts, because Yellow Card already has content for those sectors, and add commodities, mining, FMCG and pharmaceutical as the first plays prove out. The account research would run as an n8n workflow using Clay for enrichment and Claude for a short account brief: which local currencies the group earns in, recent filings or news about trapped cash, and who the treasurer and finance leads are. I built this kind of research and enrichment flow for five FunnelEnvy clients at once.

In the same month I would set up outbound infrastructure on separate sending domains, with the email authentication records (SPF, DKIM and DMARC) published, the inboxes warmed, and a check for broken merge fields before any send. I once caught a provider that had activated sending domains without publishing those records. Mail would have landed in spam with no visible error.

Days 31 to 60: fix the proof, then run the first plays

The first piece of work is a corridor cost worksheet that replaces the unsourced 8.3 percent figure. A treasurer enters a transfer size and corridor, sees every assumption, and gets a range with Yellow Card's own pricing stated or marked as quoted on request. It gives the paid ads and the outbound something a chief financial officer can verify, and it becomes the smaller next step Gap 7 lacks. I would test whether it beats "Speak to an expert" on conversion before building anything larger.

I would also write one proof page with definitions: what "countries supported," "countries reached" and "licensed jurisdictions" each mean, which licence covers which corridor, the partner bank for the dollar account once Yellow Card is ready to name it, and links to the security documents. That page would sit behind every ad and email.

Next come the plays, run in three channels at small budgets, with sales agreeing the account list first. On paid, I would run LinkedIn ads against the named account lists with account-level matching, plus paid search on terms a treasurer uses when the pain is live, which I would validate with keyword data before spending, and retargeting for visitors who reached the worksheet or the proof page. I run these platforms myself. On account-based marketing (ABM), I would run one sequenced play per tier: tier one gets a researched, account-specific message to three roles (treasurer, finance lead, chief financial officer) with ads shown to the same accounts. On outbound, I would write the sequences with sales, test two angles, and track replies by role.

Routing and scoring would go live in this window. An n8n workflow would score a lead from account tier and engagement, write it to Salesforce, and alert the account owner when someone from a tier one account opens the worksheet.

Days 61 to 90: read the stages, cut, and automate the reporting

I would read each channel by stage conversion, cost per lead and cost per MQL, move budget from what stalls to what advances, and report which stage broke for anything that underperformed. Weekly reporting would be automated, so the numbers reach sales and the leadership team without a manual pull. I would draft nurture emails and event follow-up for the worksheet and proof page downloads, and coordinate with the content function on the sector pages that are missing and on how Yellow Card's articles are read by AI search.

At day 90, I would show the account list, the stage definitions, one working workflow each for research, routing and reporting, the first results by channel and what each one cost. I would not promise a pipeline figure before I see the baseline.

What I would want to learn in the first 30 days

What the sales team's current win and loss reasons are for treasury buyers, and whether the committee looks the way I inferred. Who holds the dollars, in plain terms, and what Yellow Card is permitted to say about it. Whether the 8.3 percent figure comes from internal data, in which case it can be sourced and kept. What a typical first transaction looks like, by corridor and size, and what Yellow Card charges. What is in the Salesforce and n8n setup today, and which workflows already run. What paid spend, if any, has run, and what the tracking looks like.
