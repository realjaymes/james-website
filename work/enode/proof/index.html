Enode's demand generation: a first plan

Who signs off on Enode, and an account plan that starts after Enlit

James Praise · B2B marketing, account-based marketing and GTM

The plan

Intro

Your viewpoint with Arthur D. Little says retailers need "an operating model that aligns retail, IT and energy management around the same customer promise and economics." I read that as a description of who buys Enode. Three teams have to agree, and an executive signs. This page maps what each of them needs to see, then sets out the account program I would build with your account executives. I wrote it from Enode's public pages on 17 November 2026, so every claim about buyers is a hypothesis to test in month one.

Four people sign off

At a retailer, I'd expect four roles in every serious deal. Each asks a different question, and Enode has already published good proof for most of them. The gap is that the proof isn't arranged by who is asking.

WhoWhat they askProof Enode already hasWhat I'd add

For your account executives, this means one deck can split into four short follow-ups, one for each person in the deal. In an account-based program, each of those four people becomes a named contact with their own message.

The number I'd move to the front

What I found. Your customers page leads with four numbers: up to 50% lower charging costs at Vattenfall, 5 GWh shifted at Ostrom, 45% of daytime charging moved to solar at ev.energy and €821 a year saved by Tibber's top users. All four describe what the household gets.

Why it matters to you. The executive who signs cares about the retailer's own results. Enode has one of those already. In the Arthur D. Little viewpoint, EV connections at one large retailer rose 17-fold in the three months after it added a type-of-use tariff. That number sits in a blog post. I'd put it on the customers page and in the first slide of the sales deck.

What I'd do next. Ask each customer in the story library for one retailer-side number: connections, how long connected customers stay, or revenue from flexibility. That needs Enode's data and the customers' approval, so I'd start with the three accounts closest to Enode.

The account program

Your posting asks for demand from "the energy companies we most want to work with." I would build that list with your account executives in three tiers, sized by how much each account could be worth.

Tier 1, one account at a time. About 15 named retailers and utilities. Each gets a written account plan: the four people mapped by name, the trigger that makes now the right time, and the Enode customer they most resemble. Account executives and I agree every touch together.

Tier 2, small groups. Retailers grouped by market and use case, such as Nordic retailers adding home batteries or Dutch retailers moving to a virtual power plant. Each group gets one story from its closest customer, a short LinkedIn campaign aimed at the four roles, and an invitation to a small online session.

Tier 3, everyone else. The guides, the viewpoint and LinkedIn, aimed at the same four roles. When an account in this tier shows a trigger from the appendix, it moves up a tier.

Retailers like ENGIE, with 19 million residential contracts, show what a Tier 1 account can be worth. The 27 customer stories give each tier a lookalike to point to.

The first play: after Enlit

Enode was at Enlit Europe in Vienna from 10 to 12 November, with a stand and a talk by Ludvig Svensson on why some flexibility programs take off and others stall. The conversations from that week are the warmest demand Enode has right now, and they cool quickly.

What I'd do in the first two weeks. Log every stand conversation in HubSpot against its account and the person's role. Send each person a follow-up matched to that role, using the four rows above. Then invite the whole group to a short online session built on Ludvig's talk, which gives everyone who missed it a reason to come back.

How I'd judge it. Meetings booked and pipeline created from Enlit accounts within 60 days, tracked as their own campaign. Whatever that shows sets the plan for the next big event, E-world in Essen in February: book meetings before the event, not after.

The AI workflow behind it

Your posting asks for AI workflows for account research, buying-group mapping, outreach and reporting. Here is the one I would build first. An agent reads each Tier 1 account's public sources, including tariff pages, press releases, job posts and LinkedIn. It drafts the account plan and a first message for each of the four roles. An account executive or I approve every message before it goes out, and the approved plan is written into HubSpot.

The same agent checks Tier 2 and Tier 3 accounts each week for the triggers in the appendix. That way a small team can watch hundreds of accounts and only spend time on the ones that move.

The first 90 days

Days 1 to 30: learn the buyers. Sit in on account executive calls, read won and lost deals in HubSpot, and talk with five customers about who signed and why. Run the Enlit follow-up. Agree the Tier 1 list and the pipeline target with Fredrik.

Days 31 to 60: build the program. Write the four role messages and the deck that splits by role. Put the 17-fold number on the customers page. Start the research agent on Tier 1, and launch the first Tier 2 group.

Days 61 to 90: measure and plan 2027. Report meetings and pipeline from target accounts in HubSpot. Set the 2027 roadmap with Fredrik, including budget, channels and the events worth going to.

How I'd measure it. Meetings with target accounts, pipeline from target accounts, and how many of the four roles we reach in each Tier 1 account. Pipeline is the number that matters. The role count is the early sign that pipeline is coming.
