Built for Flex

Competitive Positioning Brief

Draft one · public sources only

Written from outside Flex, using only public sources. Every claim that needs Flex's internal data to stand up is marked proof needed rather than guessed.

Why this document exists

Flex holds two official positions on its own competitive set, and they point in opposite directions.

In March 2026, Head of Product Jared Thomas published We Don't Sell Credit Here on Flex's own resource hub. Its position is unambiguous: "We are not up against Brex or Ramp or Mercury or Bill.com." The argument is that Flex is creating a category, owner finance, and sells behavioral change rather than products.

In August 2026, the job description for Flex's first product marketer asked that person to own competitive positioning "in a market where Ramp, Brex, and Bill.com are all fighting for the same mental real estate."

Both are correct. They operate at different altitudes, and nobody has written down which one applies where.

Category narrative is what Flex says when it controls the frame. Competitive positioning is what a rep says when the prospect has already put Ramp on the table and control of the frame is gone.

A company that only has the first sends reps into deals unarmed. A company that only has the second concedes the category it is trying to build. So this brief does both: it keeps the category claim intact and gives revenue teams something to say when a prospect names a competitor.

Part 1 — The competitive set changed and the messaging has not caught up

Brex is no longer an independent company. Capital One announced its acquisition on 22 January 2026 and closed it on 7 April 2026 at $5.15 billion, roughly 60% below Brex's $12.3 billion valuation in 2021. Pedro Franceschi continues to lead Brex inside Capital One.

This matters more to Flex than to anyone else in the category, because Flex's entire premise is that owners are "stuck with outdated banks and fragmented tools." One of the three competitors named in Flex's own job description is now part of a large bank. That is not a talking point Flex has to manufacture. It happened.

It also cuts the other way, and the brief should say so. Capital One brings Brex a balance sheet, a banking charter, and a consumer brand owners already recognize. In eighteen months, "Brex, backed by Capital One" may be a stronger competitor than Brex was alone. The window where the acquisition reads as a retreat is open now and will not stay open.

Ramp is the real competitor. Series F at a $44 billion valuation in 2026, revenue reportedly doubling toward $1 billion, widely expected to go public. After the Brex sale, Ramp is effectively the one independent player at scale, and competitive investment should be weighted heavily toward it.

Part 2 — Where the category claim is actually true

The claim to defend is not "we have more products." It is that Ramp, Brex and Bill.com all stop at the company boundary, and the owner does not.

The row that matters is the second one. A $40M owner-operator is making one financial decision, not two, and every competitor in the set forces that person to hold the personal half somewhere else. That is the category claim, and it survives contact with a feature comparison because it is not a feature comparison.

The discipline that follows: never defend Flex on Ramp's axis. Flex will lose a card-rewards-and-ERP-integrations comparison against a company with a $44 billion valuation and a free tier. It wins by changing what is being compared.

Part 3 — Battlecards

One card per competitor. Never merged into a grid, because a rep needs the one card for who they are actually up against.

Battlecard: Ramp

Last verified 2026-09-16 · Applies across the full $3M–$200M band, most often at the lower end

Ramp in one line. The best-funded independent spend management platform in the market, strongest with cost-conscious US companies that want a free corporate card, flat cashback and deep ERP integrations, sold to a finance team.

Why we win. The owner's personal side is in scope and no competitor serves it proof needed: a named customer who consolidated. Credit that does not flinch when cash dips, since lumpy revenue is normal for owner-operated businesses proof needed: limit-stability policy in writing. And underwriting speed, where Flex has publicly cited AI-driven credit decisions moving from 25 days to 48 hours.

Handling objections.

  • "Ramp is free, you are not." The free tier covers card and basic expense management. Multi-entity, deeper ERP and global reimbursements sit on paid tiers, and the personal side is not available at any price. Compare the whole stack being replaced, not one line of it.
  • "Ramp has better ERP integrations." Probably true today, and worth conceding directly. Then move: how much of what you are solving lives outside the accounting system entirely?
  • "We already use Ramp and it's fine." Not a deal to fight head on. The opening is the personal side and the credit line. Ask what happens to their limit in a slow quarter.
  • "Ramp is about to IPO, they're the safe choice." Scale is real. Also ask who at Ramp is responsible for their personal balance sheet. Nobody is, by design.

Questions to ask. When you last had a slow quarter, what happened to your credit line? Where does your personal financial picture sit today, and who sees both halves? How many entities are you running, and how many logins does that take?

Pricing. Ramp: free tier; Plus around $15 per user per month plus a platform fee; Enterprise custom, with platform fees reported from $0 to $15,000+ per month. Sourced 2026-09-16, reverify quarterly. Flex: proof needed, pricing is not public

Third-party validation. Ramp holds 4.8 on G2 across 2,000+ reviews and 3.5 on Trustpilot. That gap usually means satisfied daily users alongside unhappy buyers who needed support and could not reach it. Support access is a real soft spot.

Battlecard: Brex (now Capital One)

Last verified 2026-09-16 · Upper end of the band, and owners whose companies took institutional funding

Brex in one line. A corporate card and spend platform built for funded and mid-market companies, acquired by Capital One in April 2026 and now operating inside a large consumer bank.

Why we win. Flex's founding argument just got a live example, because a competitor became the thing Flex positions against. Brex has already walked away from smaller customers once, in June 2022, which is a matter of record. And Flex's roadmap is still set by the people building it proof needed: a win where acquisition uncertainty was the stated reason.

Handling objections.

  • "Capital One backing makes Brex safer." Fair, and worth saying so rather than arguing. Then: acquisitions redirect roadmaps, and Brex's history with smaller customers is public. Whose priorities win when they conflict?
  • "We're already on Brex." Don't attack the card. Ask what it does with the personal side and the private credit need.

Questions to ask. What have you noticed change since the Capital One deal closed? Were you a Brex customer before 2022?

Watch item. This card has the shortest shelf life of the three. Reverify within one quarter. If Capital One integrates a consumer banking relationship into Brex's offering, the personal-side gap Flex owns today narrows, and this card needs rewriting rather than updating.

Battlecard: Bill.com

Last verified 2026-09-16 · A workflow competitor, not a platform competitor

Bill.com in one line. Accounts payable and receivable automation, distributed largely through accountants and bookkeepers, competing with one Flex product line rather than the platform.

Why we win. Flex absorbs the workflow instead of sitting beside it, so that's one less integration and one less bill. AP pain is usually a timing problem, and Bill.com can move an invoice but cannot fund it, which is what Bill Pay Later and working capital address proof needed: a customer who replaced Bill.com plus a credit line. And the cost stacks: ACH at $0.59, mailed checks at $1.99 and card payments at 2.9% sit on top of a $45 to $89 per-seat fee.

Handling objections.

  • "Our accountant set us up on Bill.com." The accountant is a stakeholder, not an obstacle. Bring them in early; the argument that wins is fewer systems to reconcile.
  • "Bill.com does AP better." Concede depth where it's true. Then ask whether the payables problem is really a workflow problem or a cash-timing one.

Questions to ask. Who owns the Bill.com relationship, you or your accountant? When you delay paying a vendor, is it because the approval is slow or because the cash isn't there yet?

Part 4 — What's missing, and how the first 30 days fix it

This draft was built entirely from public sources, which means the highest-value sections are the weakest ones. That's the honest state of any competitive brief written from outside, and it's worth naming rather than papering over.

Every proof needed marker falls into one of four buckets, and each has a specific internal source.

Win/loss reality. Every "why we win" claim here is inferred from public positioning. The job description mentions Apollo call-recording data, which is the right source. The first pass would code recent closed deals into the standard taxonomy of price, product gap, competitor advantage, timing, no decision and sales experience, then keep only what three or more independent interviews support. A single interview is an anecdote.

Named customer proof. The job description states there are no formal case studies. Three named owner stories, one per revenue tier, would unblock the website, the collateral and lifecycle campaigns at the same time. That's the highest-leverage missing asset in the company.

Flex's own pricing and qualification criteria. Not public, and a battlecard without them is incomplete.

Which competitor actually shows up. This brief assumes Ramp is the main threat, reasoning from funding, scale and independence after the Brex sale. That assumption should be tested against real pipeline data in week one. If Flex is actually losing to regional banks and incumbent relationships rather than to fintech, then all three cards are aimed at the wrong target and the priority changes completely.

That last one is the question worth asking before anything else gets built.

A note on how this was put together

This is a first draft built from outside the company, so the most useful thing it can demonstrate is not polish but method.

The structure follows the battlecard format in the competitive intelligence skill I maintain publicly in marketingagentskills, which is the same structure I'd use inside Flex rather than a one-off shape invented for an application.

Two choices are worth explaining. The first is the proof needed convention, which is deliberate. It would have been easy to write plausible win rates and confident customer claims and produce a document that reads as finished. A battlecard with invented numbers gets used once on a live call, fails, and is never opened again. Marking the gaps is what makes the rest trustworthy.

The second is conceding points to competitors in writing. Ramp's ERP integrations are better. Capital One's backing is a real advantage for Brex. Bill.com does deeper AP. Reps already know this, because prospects tell them. A card that pretends otherwise loses credibility on the first objection, and reps stop reaching for it.
