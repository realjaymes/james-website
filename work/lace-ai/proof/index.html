One Name, One Reason: A Category and Competitive Brief for Lace

James Praise · Marketing and GTM Operator

I wrote this from Lace's public pages and its competitors' sites on 11 November 2026. I have no inside data and no affiliation with Lace. Every claim about a website links to the page it came from. Anything that needs your deal data or customer numbers is marked proof needed.

The read

Lace has five names today

A home services owner who meets Lace in three places reads three different descriptions of the same product. These are the five I found on your own pages.

"The AI Revenue System for the Trades"The headline on the lace.ai homepage.
"Revenue automation for Call Centers"The page title of the same homepage, which is what Google shows in search results.
"AI call center solutions"The partners section further down the homepage.
"AI Customer Engagement Platform"The About section of the careers pages, including this role's posting.
"AI-powered revenue intelligence software for the call center in the trades"The tagline on Lace's LinkedIn page.

Each one is accurate. Together they make a buyer work out what Lace is. They also make your new demand generation and events hires pick a name themselves. The posting asks the first PMM to own "positioning and naming as the portfolio grows", so this is where I'd start.

What the rivals say

Two direct rivals already share one name. Avoca calls itself "The AI Front Office for Service Businesses" and Sameday calls itself "The AI front office for home services." Avoca also announced a $125M Series B at a $1B valuation.

"Front office" describes answering the phone. Lace does that, and it does something the phrase leaves out: its AI Revenue Engine scores every call and coaches the human team. Your help center says many customers run AI and human CSRs side by side. That is the opening.

The name I'd test

Lace is the AI call center for the trades. AI agents answer every call, and Lace scores every call, AI or human, on whether it booked the job.

"Call center" is the owner's own word, and Lace already uses it in its page title, its closing line ("The AI secret behind call centers for the trades") and its LinkedIn tagline. It also covers both products, which "front office" does not. I'd treat this as a hypothesis and test it in customer and lost-deal interviews before it goes on the site.

Message hierarchy

One line: Every call answered, every call scored, more jobs booked.

Pillar 1, never miss a call. The AI CSR answers around the clock and books straight into ServiceTitan. Proof: Tommy Mello of A1 Garage says on the homepage that it is "booking over 85% of the calls."

Pillar 2, make your people better. The Revenue Engine finds the missed sale in every human call and turns it into coaching. Proof: the PipeDreams story cut call reviewers from six or seven people to one or two.

Pillar 3, pay for what you use. $0.99 a minute and no monthly fee. Proof needed: average cost per booked job across customers proof needed.

Handling objections

"Avoca is the bigger, safer company." Bigger, yes. Ask what they need the vendor to do in month three. Lace only builds for the trades, and the same team that sets up your playbook changes it the same day.

"We just want the phone answered." Then start with the AI CSR and pay by the minute. The scoring comes with it, so you'll see why the calls it didn't book were lost.

"Per-minute pricing feels unpredictable." Show cost per booked job instead. proof needed

Questions to ask

  • How many of your calls do people still take today, and who listens to them?
  • What field software do you run, and is ServiceTitan staying?
  • When you last lost a booking, how did you find out why?

The price story

Avoca and Netic publish no price. Lace has one, and it sits in a help article. I'd move it onto the site and show it as cost per booked job. For example, if a booking call lasts four minutes, it costs $3.96. Your real average call length would replace that example proof needed.

First 90 days

  • Month one: interview ten customers and five lost deals, sit in on sales calls, and agree the category name with you and Boris.
  • Month two: ship battlecards for Avoca, Sameday and ServiceTitan, plus a one-page category sheet your demand generation and events hires use.
  • Month three: publish the price story and two comparison pages, and turn three customer cards into full stories.

I'd track win rate against each named competitor, the share of deals where sales records the competitor, and how often reps open each card.
