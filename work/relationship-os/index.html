Side Project — Relationship OS

A relationship system that tells me who I've gone quiet on, and gives me something real to say

One structured note per person, a dashboard that flags who is overdue, and a Claude skill that researches every new contact and drafts outreach in my own voice. I review and send every message myself.

What It Is

Relationship OS is the system I use to manage my professional relationships. It holds one structured note per person in Obsidian, a dashboard that shows who is due for a touch, and a Claude skill that runs the whole loop. When I add someone, the skill researches their career history and recent activity through the browser before the note is even finished. It currently tracks 141 people across six relationship types: past employers, recruiters, referral partners, senior operators, peers, and prospects.

Why I Built It

Most of my best work has come through people rather than cold outbound. Even so, those relationships went quiet without me noticing. When I did reach out, the message often read as generic because I had nothing specific to say.

Boardy, an AI that introduces people to each other, connected me with Daniel Johnson, the founder of We Scale Startups. On our call he showed me the Notion system he uses to manage his relationships. I keep everything I know in Obsidian, so I built my own version there the same week and connected it to the rest of my AI stack. Another operator sends a short five-part update to mentors every half year, and people he assumed were too busy write back. I combined both habits into one system that fits how I work.

What It Does

  2. 2B Close or wider network?

3 WRITE AND SEND

  1. 3A Draft the messageWritten fresh in my voice from what is happening with them
  2. 3B I review and sendThe system never sends. I send every message myself

Two cadence tiers. Close relationships get a personal message about once a month. My wider network gets a segmented update once a quarter, sent in the same fixed week each time.

Automatic research on add. New contacts start with their career history and recent posts already filled in, so the first message can be specific.

Overdue tracking. Each person has a silence threshold, and the dashboard lists everyone past it.

Drafts from real signals. 1:1 messages are written fresh in my own voice from what is currently happening with that person. Stored templates go stale.

Segmented broadcasts. Recruiters, past employers, and past clients each get their own quarterly update: why I'm writing, wins, challenges, what's next, and a real question back.

Connected to the rest of my stack. Claude reads the calendar invite Boardy sends for each intro and opens a note for the new person. A Gmail script checks every contact against my real email history, so each note shows when we last actually spoke.

It never sends anything. Every draft gets my review, and I send it myself. That was a deliberate design choice.

What It Taught Me

A contact list is easy to build, but a reason to reach out is hard to find. The value sits in the research and signal layer, because that is what turns "checking in" into a message worth replying to. A slower cadence also holds up better than a faster one. Weekly one-way contact reads as needy, and most relationships don't produce new signals that often. None of this depends on Obsidian. Anyone can build the same system in Notion or a spreadsheet with one note per person, a silence threshold for each one, and a real reason before every message.
