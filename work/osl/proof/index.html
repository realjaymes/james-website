An Africa go-to-market for OSL: a first draft

OSL in Africa: start with the money Africa already sends to Asia

James Praise · GTM, partnerships and revenue

The draft

Intro

Your Country Manager (Africa) posting asks for someone to choose the markets, corridors and partners, and own the regional revenue. I wrote a first version from OSL's public pages and public trade data on 17 November 2026. It says where I would start, which partners I would rank first, what African buyers need to see, and what I would do in 90 days.

It is a starting point. Month one would test it against your own volume data and the partner pipeline OSL's Lagos channel team already holds.

Where I'd start: the import bill

China is Africa's largest trading partner, and most of that trade is Africa buying. In 2025 China sold $225 billion of goods to Africa and bought $123 billion back, according to the Centre for African Studies at Nanyang Technological University (NTU).

Nigeria shows it most clearly. China supplied 31% of Nigeria's imports in the last quarter of 2025, by naira value, its largest source by far (National Bureau of Statistics figures, via Zawya). Every one of those shipments needs a payment to a supplier abroad.

So I would lead with one buyer and one route: African businesses paying suppliers in China and Hong Kong, starting in Nigeria.

Why OSL wins that corridor

A supplier payment from Lagos to Guangzhou has two ends. African ramps such as Yellow Card and Busha already turn naira into stablecoins, digital tokens that hold the value of a US dollar. The other end, paying a Chinese supplier through licensed Asian rails, is where a Hong Kong firm licensed by the Securities and Futures Commission has an edge over African firms, because Asian banks and payout partners can work with it.

OSL has already started building that bridge. In May 2026 Yellow Card joined OSL's Stable Alliance, which settles Africa to Asia-Pacific payments in USDGO. The aim is to cut the cash each firm has to park in every country. That makes African ramps likely partners for OSL.

Gap 1: Africa is a line in the licence list

On the BizPay page on 17 November 2026, Africa appears once, in "50+ licences across APAC, EMEA, the Americas and Africa". The rails named are SEPA, FPS, CHAPS, ACH, Fedwire and SWIFT. The payout icons show euros, Hong Kong dollars and US dollars. The licence strip lists Hong Kong, Japan, Indonesia, Bermuda and Australia.

An importer in Lagos reading that page finds no naira, no African rail and no African customer. Why it matters: partners selling OSL in Africa have nothing on OSL's own site to point at, so every meeting starts from zero.

Gap 2: the importer has no use case

BizPay's "Built for real-world money movement" tabs are Payroll, CFD (contract for difference) trading, Digital Entertainment and PSPs (payment service providers). The worked fee example sends US dollars to euros. Supplier payments appear only in the FAQ, as one item in a list.

The buyer with the largest African flow, the importer, has no tab of their own. A fifth tab, "Importers paying Asian suppliers", with a naira to yuan example, would give partners and buyers the same story.

Partners, ranked by what they bring

The posting lists banks, payment providers, fintechs, merchants and liquidity providers. Each brings something different, so I would rank them by customers reachable, monthly volume and time to the first transaction.

The ramps come first because the Stable Alliance already exists. Payment providers and fintechs come next, because one signed partner brings many importers. Banks take longest, so I would start those talks early and expect them to land later.

A 90-day plan

Days 1 to 30: learn and choose. Read OSL's volume by corridor, the Lagos team's pipeline and what Compliance has approved. Talk to ten importers and the Stable Alliance partners. Rank five or six corridors and agree the first one with leadership.

Days 31 to 60: equip and sign. Ship the importer one-pager and a partner deck. Agree one joint offer per partner type. Aim for the first three partners live on the chosen corridor.

Days 61 to 90: prove and report. Run the first importer pilots through partners and report weekly volume per partner to senior management. Bring Compliance the evidence for the next market.

Licences and regulator work stay with OSL's Compliance and Legal teams. Early volume runs through partners who already hold local licences.

Appendix: the importer one-pager, first draft

Headline: Pay your suppliers in China in minutes, not days.

Who it is for: businesses in Nigeria and across Africa that buy goods from China and Hong Kong.

How it works: you pay in naira through a licensed local partner. The partner converts it to USDGO, a regulated US dollar stablecoin. OSL settles it and pays your supplier in yuan or Hong Kong dollars through licensed rails in Asia.

Illustration using BizPay's published ranges: take a $50,000 supplier invoice. At the 3% to 7% BizPay quotes for a bank wire, the payment costs $1,500 to $3,500 and takes three to five days. At BizPay's 0.8% to 1.2%, the same payment costs $400 to $600 and settles in minutes. Actual rates depend on the corridor and partner.

Call to action: ask your payment partner about OSL, or speak to OSL's Africa team.

Appendix: what I'd measure

  • Monthly volume per corridor and per partner.
  • Active business customers, and the share that send a second payment within 30 days.
  • Partner time to first transaction.
  • Revenue per corridor after conversion and payout costs.

Appendix: what I checked

  • OSL's homepage and BizPay page, read live in Chrome on 17 November 2026. I did not open the OSL Pay or Stable Alliance pages. Africa content may exist there.
  • OSL's 2025 annual results and the first-half 2026 filing.
  • Trade figures from NTU and Zawya.
  • I do not know OSL's internal volume, licences in progress or partner pipeline. Each would change the order above.
