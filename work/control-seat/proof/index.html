A positioning teardown for Control Seat

Most of the public record describes a platform that runs factories. Your website sells predictive maintenance.

James Praise · Marketing and GTM Operator

I wrote this from public pages only, all opened on October 14, 2026: your Y Combinator listing, the Founderland article of September 19, your LinkedIn page, your X profile and your website, including each of the 18 pages in its sitemap. I did not see the product, the login or any customer data.

1. What each page says about Control Seat

Each of these descriptions is accurate on its own page. Read together, they describe a company that sells to different people for different reasons. Here is each one as it stood on October 14, 2026.

Three buyers are named across these pages: a maintenance team (your website), a controls engineer or system integrator (your Y Combinator listing) and a marine fleet (your LinkedIn overview). Only the first is spoken to on the homepage.

2. What a plant buyer meets

Take someone who hears about Control Seat at a conference. Your LinkedIn page shows Warren speaking on a "Modern SCADA for Utilities" panel in Atlanta, and a repost of his Grand Rapids post saying PLCs, SCADA, historians, alarm systems and predictive maintenance are "unified under one platform." That person types in controlseat.com and meets a first screen about vibration, ultrasound and temperature sensors. Nothing links the two.

Now take a controls engineer who read "replace Ignition, WinCC and FactoryTalk" on your Y Combinator page. Your connected data page tells them the software can "monitor without replacing your existing controls," and your pricing page answers "Do we have to connect our PLCs or SCADA?" with "No." Both lines are fine for a maintenance manager. For someone who came to evaluate a control system, they read like a different product.

Narrowing to predictive maintenance may be the right strategy. Maintenance teams can buy sensors this quarter, and Precision Associates is a published example. The terms say the software reads plant data and does not write values back to control systems, and a plant that has been burned by software glitches will want to hear that. The gap is that your own channels do not agree on whether it is the whole company or one module of a larger one.

3. What I'd lead with

I would lead with predictive maintenance, because it is what a plant can buy today and the only part with a published customer that I could find. I would name the platform once, as the place it is heading, and I would pick one name for each thing.

For maintenance teams at manufacturers who learn about a failing machine only after it stops production, Control Seat is predictive maintenance that starts with wireless sensors, reads the plant data you already collect and never writes to your controls. It is one module of FactoryOS, the platform Control Seat is building to bring equipment data, monitoring and control into one system.

One question decides what changes, and only you can answer it. If the SCADA and Virtual PLC modules the article describes are live, your website undersells three of your four modules, and the line above is the start of fixing that. If they are paused, the Y Combinator listing, the LinkedIn headline and the X bio are the pages to change, and the website is already right. Either way, the Precision Associates deployment should have one name. Today it is FactoryOS on LinkedIn and predictive maintenance on your blog.

4. A homepage test for this week

Keep your current headline. It names a concrete outcome in plain words. The test is one added line beneath it, with everything else unchanged, against the page as it stands.

The line I'd run first

Predictive maintenance is one module of FactoryOS, the platform Control Seat is building for the whole plant floor. It connects the buyer who reads the coverage to the product the website sells, and it names the platform once without asking a maintenance manager to evaluate a control system.

Two alternates worth testing against it

Read-only. Control Seat reads your plant data and never writes to your controls. This is already in your terms. Moving it to the homepage turns a legal sentence into the reassurance a cautious plant is looking for.
Precision Associates monitors vibration, ultrasound and temperature on critical equipment with Control Seat. Taken from your September 30 post. A named customer on the first screen answers "does anyone use this" before a visitor has to ask.

Measure demo bookings and quote requests per visitor, since the homepage sends people to both a demo booking link and a quote form. The conference and Founderland traffic is the audience this test is for, so segment those visits if your analytics allow.

5. How to know whether any of this is right

I built this from the outside and have no internal data. Treat it as a set of hypotheses. Here are four ways to check them, fastest first.

Read your own demo and quote notes

Look for the question "do you replace our SCADA?" or "is this the FactoryOS thing?" in your Calendly notes and quote requests. Each time it appears, a visitor hit the gap described above. A few of them in a month is enough to act on.

Ask the Precision Associates team how they describe you

Their words for what Control Seat does are the best homepage copy you can get. Note whether they say sensors, predictive maintenance or platform, and use whichever they reach for first.

Run a five second test with maintenance managers

Show eight or ten of them the current first screen and the one with the added line. Take it away and ask what the company sells and what it plans to sell. If the answers match the page, it works.

Run the homepage test above against real traffic

Pair it with a source you control, such as a conference follow-up email or a post that links to the site. Watch who books, since the first customers set the story everyone else hears.

What I couldn't check

The Founderland article describes the platform from its own reporting and says connectors for Rockwell FactoryTalk and Siemens WinCC appear on your website as "coming soon." I did not find that on any website page on October 14. LinkedIn shows post ages as "1w" and "3mo," so those dates are approximate. Warren's launch post is quoted from its repost on your company page, not from his own profile.
