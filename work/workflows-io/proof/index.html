1. ICP and tiers: who Pylon writes to first

Pylon calls itself the only agentic support platform purpose-built for B2B. Its case studies show who feels the problem. Hightouch supports customers in more than 300 shared Slack channels. At Wispr Flow, one person handled about 500 support tickets a week, and a six-person team now handles more than 2,000, according to its case study.

The engineer sets the ideal customer profile (ICP) and the tiers with the client's team in week one. I tier by account value, which here means the size of the support problem. These numbers are my starting point from the case studies, and the first call would correct them.

2. The qualifier test: does the prompt recognize real customers?

The qualifier is a short prompt an agent runs on every account before it enters a campaign. This is the version I would test. It reads only the company's own public pages.

Qualifier prompt, version 2

Company: {{name}}, {{domain}}. Read only this company's own public pages. Answer PASS or FAIL, then give one line of evidence quoted from a page.

PASS only if all three are true.

1. It sells software or an application programming interface (API) to businesses, and businesses pay for it through a team, business or enterprise plan or a sales contact.

2. Customers use it every day or in production, so they will ask for help often.

3. The site shows an ongoing business relationship. That means a support route (help center, support link, community, shared Slack or status page). It can also be a sales-led enterprise path with contract terms or a service-level agreement (SLA), or named business customers.

FAIL if the company is a consumer product, hardware or a service business.

The engineer tests the qualifier on the client's closed-won deals first, and 95% or more should pass. I do not have Pylon's deal list, so I used 19 customers from its public customers page and read each company's own site.

Version 1 also required a visible support route on the public pages. It passed 12 of 19 (63%). Seven real customers failed. Six of them keep support behind a login or a sales form: Linear, Together AI, Retool, AssemblyAI, Applied Intuition and CodeRabbit. The seventh, Wispr Flow, leads with a consumer app, yet it sells team and enterprise plans.

I changed two rules. A sales-led enterprise path with contract terms, or named business customers, now counts as a business relationship. A consumer app passes if it also sells a paid team or enterprise plan. Version 2 passed 19 of 19. The appendix shows every account.

That result is weaker than it looks. I changed the rules after seeing the failures, and a test on real customers only shows the prompt does not reject good accounts. As a spot check, Peloton, a consumer fitness brand, fails the first rule. Before a live list, I would run a fresh set of customers and a set of non-customers.

3. Signals: the three worth acting on

I would act on three signals. Each one points to a growing support load.

A support or customer success job postA company hiring its first support or success people has outgrown founder-led support. The source is the company's own careers page, checked weekly. Tier 1 and Tier 2 accounts get the first email within days of the post.
A funding roundNew money usually brings new customers within months and a larger support load. The source is the company's own announcement. LangChain, for example, states on its homepage that it raised a $300 million Series C at a $3 billion valuation.
A competing support tool named in the company's own pagesA job post or help center that names Zendesk, Front or Intercom marks an account that may be ready to switch. Pylon says more than 150 companies have migrated from older platforms, and one case study is titled How Sardine replaced Zendesk in 5 Days.

The engineer decides which signal goes into which campaign. A hiring signal goes to the head of support. A migration signal gets its own email.

4. Copy: one email by hand, two versions by agent

The engineer writes the Tier 1 email by hand, because that segment has the clearest proof point. Agents then adapt it to the other segments.

Tier 1: head of support at a company with 100+ customer Slack channels

Subject: 300 Slack channels, one queue

{{first_name}}, {{company}} supports customers in shared Slack channels, and every new customer adds one more to watch.

Hightouch reached more than 300 of them. Its team now gets every support question into one queue, and says it saves about 100 hours a month finding and tracking questions across channels.

Customers stay in Slack, and your team works from one queue. Would 15 minutes next week be useful?

The Hightouch numbers come from its case study, which Pylon published. The agent adapts the email for two segments.

Tier 2: head of customer success at a Series A developer tool

{{first_name}}, when customers reach {{company}} in Slack, email and chat, the first person to see a question is often luck.

Wispr Flow's support work grew from one person handling about 500 tickets a week to six people handling more than 2,000. Its team says it can now promise response times because it sees what is coming in.

Would a short look at how your channels would work be useful?

Migration signal: head of support whose own pages name Zendesk or Front

{{first_name}}, {{company}}'s help center runs on {{tool}}.

Sardine replaced Zendesk with Pylon in five days, according to its case study, and Pylon says more than 150 companies have moved from older platforms. If a switch is on your list this year, I can show you what the first week looks like.

The engineer checks these in every agent version:

  • Every number and customer name still matches its case study.
  • The customer named is not a competitor of the recipient, and the client has approved using the name.
  • The opening line matches the signal that put the account on the list.
  • The email asks one question.
  • No claim has crept in that the client has not published.

5. Pre-flight: the checks before anything goes live

The build and the basic checks are automated. The engineer runs the last check by hand, in this order:

  1. Send five emails to my own inboxes at different mail providers, and read each one filled in.
  2. Scan the list for blank or odd first names and company names.
  3. Remove invalid addresses, and anyone on the client's customer, open-deal or do-not-contact lists in its customer relationship management (CRM) system.
  4. Confirm the sending domains have their email authentication records and have finished warm-up.
  5. Check that each inbox's daily send count is inside its warm-up limit.
  6. Confirm each customer named in an email is public and approved.
  7. Confirm replies reach the client's inbox and the unsubscribe link works.

What goes first

Tier 1 goes first, with a small batch of about 40 contacts and the hand-written email only. A small batch shows delivery and the angle before more volume goes out. The 40 is my number, to set with the client. The other tiers follow once I have read the first replies.

6. Replies: what I read in week two, and when to double down

In week two the engineer reads every reply in full, not only the labels agents put on them. The order is delivery first, meaning bounces and spam complaints. Then come positive replies by segment, and last the wording of the negative ones. A reply such as "we are on Zendesk until March" is a timing signal, and "wrong person" often names the right one.

My rule for doubling down is to raise a segment's volume when three or more people ask for a call and their replies describe the problem the email named. If replies are positive but vague, I keep the volume and test a sharper angle. The three is a starting rule to set with the client, and it is not a benchmark.

7. The weekly call: one agenda that spends the time on strategy

Agents draft the agenda, the recap and the weekly report from the client workspace. The engineer reads and corrects them before the call, so the call itself is for decisions. One 30-minute agenda:

  1. Five minutes on the recap, and the one number that changed.
  2. Ten minutes on which segment to double, hold or stop, and why.
  3. Ten minutes on three reply quotes word for word, and what they say about the offer.
  4. Five minutes on next week's list, and the one decision the client owes us, such as approving a new customer name for emails.
