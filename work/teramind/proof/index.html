A brief for Teramind

One Story From Campaign to Contract

James Praise · Marketing and GTM Operator

Everything here comes from Teramind's public site, its March launch, its Shadow AI Report and Microsoft's own documentation, checked live in September 2026. I haven't seen closed-won or closed-lost data, call recordings or the current sales deck. Anything that needs internal evidence is marked proof needed, so the gaps stay visible.

The finding

Teramind is telling three true AI stories at once

In six months, Teramind has put three different framings of the AI problem into market. Each one is defensible on its own.

March launch: governance lets you say yesThe AI Governance launch quotes Isaac Kohen: "The answer isn't less AI. It's governed AI." This is an enablement story, pitched at leaders who want adoption with guardrails.
The research: the sanctioned tool is the riskThe Shadow AI Report 2026 leads with 89% of workplace AI use escaping governance "not through rogue apps, but through the approved platforms organizations deployed and trusted." The homepage banner repeats it.
The product page: unsanctioned AI is the new insiderThe AI Governance page opens with "AI Is the New Insider Threat" and names "unsanctioned desktop apps, local LLMs, and browser extensions" as the problem.

A buyer who reads the report, clicks through to the product and then sits in a demo gets three versions of the problem. Each is true, but only one should lead. My hypothesis is that the research framing is the strongest lead, because it's the one Microsoft Purview and network DLP can't easily claim. If the risk sits inside tools the company already approved, a blocklist can't catch it. Only a record of what people actually did inside those tools can.

Where the story splits below the fold

The campaign layer is sharp. The pages, pricing and proof that follow it are where a security buyer and an operations buyer start hearing the same sentences.

Pricing puts the newest category inside a productivity tierOn the pricing page, "Agentic AI Governance" is one line item in the UAM plan, which is described as "Best for comprehensive productivity optimization and security detection & investigation." Insider risk management and AI governance don't have their own entry point, so the packaging narrative has to be told by sales in every deal.
A security page closes on productivity analyticsThe ChatGPT DLP page is written for a buyer worried about data leaving through prompts. Its "Core Features" block ends with "Active Vs. Idle Time Logs" and "Unproductive Work Time Analysis." For a Chief Information Security Officer (CISO), those tiles read as surveillance. For a works council or a privacy reviewer, they are an objection waiting to happen.
One proof line for every buyerThe DLP, ChatGPT DLP, Employee Monitoring and AI Governance pages all carry the same claim: "Trusted by 10,000+ organizations to improve productivity, security, compliance, and workforce analytics." The homepage title calls Teramind a "Workforce Analytics & Insider Risk Platform." Nothing on these pages tells a buyer which of the four is theirs.
Two competitive sets, two different stories about the same rivalThe AI Productivity Monitoring page compares Teramind with ActivTrak, Hubstaff, WorkTime and DTEX. The Insider Risk page positions Teramind as a way to "Complement Microsoft Purview IRM," while the role itself asks for battlecards against Purview. Both motions are real, and sales needs to know which one to run.

The platform is one sensor. The buyers are two people with different fears, and the site currently speaks to both of them in the same sentence.

The work

One message house, two buyer tracks

This is how I'd rebuild the structure, using the research framing as the umbrella and splitting everything underneath it by buyer rather than by category.

Platform claim

Draft: Teramind is the record of what people and AI actually do at work. Security teams use that record to stop data leaving. Operations teams use it to see how work gets done.

The claim is endpoint-first on purpose. It names the one asset every competitor in both sets lacks, since network and cloud tools see traffic and time trackers see activity, and Teramind's endpoint telemetry sees both. The four categories become four doors into the same record instead of four products.

A problem-led demo for the AI governance motion

The first ten minutes of a first call with a security buyer, built so the prospect sees their own problem before they see a dashboard.

  1. Minutes 0 to 2. Their tools, not ours. "Which AI tools have you approved this year?" Write the list down. Most teams name Copilot, Gemini or ChatGPT Enterprise.
  2. Minutes 2 to 4. The uncomfortable stat. Share one line from the Shadow AI Report: most ungoverned AI use runs through approved platforms. Ask how they'd know today if someone pasted a customer list into one of the tools on their list.
  3. Minutes 4 to 7. One replay. Show a single recorded session: a sanctioned AI tool, a sensitive paste, the rule that fired, the redaction. One story, start to finish. No feature tour.
  4. Minutes 7 to 9. The layer they already own. Draw their stack: Purview or network DLP sees the cloud and the browser. Show the gap Microsoft's own docs describe (see the battlecard below), then show the same event caught on the endpoint.
  5. Minutes 9 to 10. The audit question. "If your auditor asked for AI usage evidence next quarter, what would you hand them?" Open the audit trail view. Then agree the pilot scope.

Rules for the rep: never open on productivity features with this buyer, show one session replay instead of five, and let the prospect name the risk before the product does.

Battlecard: Teramind alongside Microsoft Purview

When you'll meet it: any Microsoft 365 E5 customer, or any account whose security team has already started Microsoft's AI data security setup. The default motion is to extend Purview, and the fallback is to replace it for teams that don't hold E5 licenses.

Where Purview is strong (say so)

  • It's already paid for in E5 estates, and it's native to Microsoft 365, sensitivity labels and Copilot.
  • It's privacy-conscious by design: users are pseudonymized by default, per Microsoft's Insider Risk Management setup guide.

Where Teramind extends it (from Microsoft's own documentation)

  • Visual evidence is an add-on with limits. Purview's forensic evidence feature is an opt-in add-on that is off by default. It supports Windows 10 and 11 Enterprise, requires per-user capture requests and approvals, and deletes clips after 120 days. Screen recording and live view are standard features across Teramind's plans. proof needed: confirm OS coverage, retention and approval options before a rep cites them
  • AI visibility leans on the browser and the network. Microsoft's deployment guidance for AI data security requires a browser extension for Windows users on Chrome, onboarded devices, pay-as-you-go billing for AI apps beyond Microsoft 365 Copilot, and a SASE or SSE partner for its network policy. Teramind's AI Governance page claims coverage of desktop AI apps, terminal and CLI tools, and local models on the device itself.
  • Some risk indicators cost extra. Microsoft notes that some Insider Risk Management indicators are only available with pay-as-you-go billing, so the "already included" argument has a boundary.

Landmine questions for discovery

  • "Which of your users run AI tools outside the browser, like desktop apps, IDE assistants or a terminal?"
  • "Are all your users on Windows Enterprise and licensed for E5?"
  • "How long does it take today to get approval to review what a flagged user did?"

The same structure applies to the other two named rivals, with different lead lines. Forcepoint sells classification breadth ("1,800+ Policy and Classifier Templates"), so Teramind shouldn't fight on rule counts and should lead with behavior and AI prompts instead. ActivTrak now sells "work intelligence" and says it can "track AI impact." On the operations track, the answer is that Teramind can also act on what it sees.

What I'd validate in the first 30 days

  1. Deal data before opinions. I'd tag the last two quarters of closed-won and closed-lost deals by buyer track, entry door and competitor, to see whether the two-track split matches how Teramind actually wins.
  2. Eight to ten win-loss and churn interviews, split across both tracks, recorded, with each theme traced to a message change or a roadmap note.
  3. Search and call language. I'd compare the words prospects use on calls and in search (shadow AI, insider risk, employee monitoring, DLP for ChatGPT) against the page headlines, and fix the biggest mismatch first.
  4. One page, one test. Split the ChatGPT DLP page into a security version with the productivity tiles removed, and measure demo requests against the current page.
  5. Enablement the reps choose to use. I'd ship the Purview battlecard and the demo opening above as drafts, sit in on five calls, and rewrite both from what reps actually said.

Why I built it this way

Outside in, on purpose

I built this the way a buyer meets Teramind: campaign first, then research, pages, pricing and the competitor's documentation. That order shows where a strong top-of-funnel story loses shape before it reaches a contract, which is the exact stretch this role owns.

I kept the competitive claims to what Microsoft, Forcepoint and ActivTrak publish about themselves. A battlecard that overstates a rival loses a rep's trust on the first call where the prospect knows better. Where the answer lives in Teramind's deal data, I left a marker instead of a guess.
