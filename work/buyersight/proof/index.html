A prompt test for BuyerSight

Three Prompt Versions for the JET Hotel Segment

James Praise · GTM Engineer

I built this from public information only and have no access to BuyerSight's prompts, tables or campaigns, so nothing here claims to know how your current prompts perform. I also ran it outside Clay: Python fetched each prospect's website and source article, standing in for Clay's scrape step, and an AI model ran each prompt per row, standing in for an AI column. The prompts and the table structure move into Clay unchanged. No emails were sent.

The test

What I tested and why

Your homepage features Jet Hotel Solutions as a customer. JET plans and manages low-voltage infrastructure for new-build and renovated hotels, from plans review through opening, so its buyers are the developers, owners and management companies behind hotel projects. I picked that segment because a good first line there depends on reading a project correctly, which is exactly where a generic prompt falls down.

The role asks for someone to "write and optimize AI prompts to research prospects, personalize outreach, and improve campaigns at scale" and to "document experiments, results, and learnings". So I treated it as an experiment. I sourced 25 real US hotel developers, owner-operators and management companies, each with a public 2024 to 2026 project signal. I ran three prompt versions on every row, then had a separate pass score each first line without knowing which prompt wrote it.

The prompts

Version 1: a standard prompt

This is the prompt most tables start with: research the company, then write a personalized first line.

You are a sales research assistant. Using the company website text and the news signal below, write a short research summary about {{company_name}} that a sales rep could use for outreach. Include anything interesting about the company.

Company: {{company_name}}
Website text: {{website_text}}
Signal: {{signal}}
Signal source text: {{signal_source_text}}
Write a personalized first line for a cold email to {{contact_name_or_team}} at {{company_name}}, from JET, a hotel low-voltage infrastructure and technology project management firm. Use the research below. Make it engaging and personalized.

Research: {{research}}

Every line named the right project, but most wrapped it in praise the sources never supported, and none of them connected to anything JET does. Rows without a named contact came out addressed to "The development team," as if that were a person.

Greg, the 480-room Embassy Suites and Tempo project in downtown Austin is a substantial undertaking as construction moves toward its expected early 2029 opening.

Version 2: facts only, matched to the project stage

The research step now returns structured facts: the project, its stage with a quote that proves it, the JET service that fits that stage, and a confidence flag. The first line refuses to write when confidence is low.

Role: you prepare facts for a cold email from JET (hotel low-voltage infrastructure, carrier services and guest-facing technology, managed from plans review through opening).

Use ONLY the inputs below. If a fact is not stated in them, do not write it.

Inputs:
- Company: {{company_name}}
- Website text: {{website_text}}
- Signal: {{signal}}
- Signal source text: {{signal_source_text}}

Return JSON with exactly these keys:
- "project": the single most specific hotel project named in the inputs (brand, room count, city), or null
- "stage": one of "announced_or_design", "under_construction", "recently_opened", "renovation_or_conversion", "unknown"
- "stage_evidence": the shortest exact quote from the inputs that proves the stage, or null
- "jet_pathway": "new_build_plans_review" for announced_or_design or under_construction, "renovation_survey" for renovation_or_conversion, "post_opening_tech" for recently_opened, "none" for unknown
- "hook_fact": one concrete fact a hotel owner would recognize as theirs (not a compliment, not their mission statement), or null
- "confidence": "high" if project and stage_evidence are both present, "low" otherwise
Write the first sentence of a cold email from JET to {{contact_name_or_team}} at {{company_name}}.

Research JSON: {{research}}

Rules:
- If confidence is "low" or project is null, return exactly: NO_LINE
- Name the project using the research's own words (brand, city or room count).
- Tie the sentence to the stage: for design or construction, the point where cabling, MDF/IDF and carrier decisions get locked in; for renovations, what the existing building will and won't support; for recent openings, what shows up in guest Wi-Fi once rooms fill.
- One sentence, 30 words or fewer, plain language, no question marks.
- No compliments ("impressive", "exciting", "congrats"), no exclamation marks, no claims about the company that the research doesn't contain.

Accuracy and relevance jumped, and the three rows with no project correctly returned NO_LINE. The new problem was repetition. All 22 lines came from three stock sentences, one per stage, and 14 used the same one. Every line was accurate, but sent to a list, they read as a template, and near-identical copy across a list hurts deliverability.

Greg, as the 480-room Embassy Suites and Tempo in downtown Austin moves through construction, cabling, MDF/IDF and carrier decisions are getting locked in.

Version 3: one unique detail, one consequence

Version 3 keeps the version 2 research and changes only the first line. Each line has to build on a detail unique to that project and name what it means for the building's network, cabling, carriers or Wi-Fi. The greeting moves out of the line, and stock phrases are banned.

Write the first sentence of a cold email from JET (hotel low-voltage infrastructure, carrier services and guest-facing technology, managed from plans review through opening) to someone at {{company_name}}. The greeting is added separately, so do not start with a name or "the team".

Research JSON: {{research}}
Signal: {{signal}}

Rules:
- If research confidence is "low" or project is null, return exactly: NO_LINE
- Build the sentence around ONE detail that is unique to this project and stated in the research or signal: the opening date, a dual-brand or conversion setup, a campus, convention or mixed-use location, a phased renovation, or the room count only if nothing better exists.
- Name one practical consequence of that detail for the building's network, cabling, carrier circuits or guest Wi-Fi. Examples of the kind of link wanted (do not copy them): two brands under one roof means two sets of brand technology standards in one riser plan; a conversion inside an operating hotel means cabling paths decided around a building still taking guests; a 2027 opening puts carrier orders, which take months, inside this year's schedule.
- Do not reuse a stock phrase. Write it as if this were the only email you were sending today.
- One sentence, 30 words or fewer, plain language, no question marks, no exclamation marks, no compliments, no claims the inputs don't support.

The same Austin tower from the examples above now reads like it was written for one developer:

Putting Embassy Suites and Tempo into one 30-story tower means two brand technology programs must share a coordinated riser, network and carrier plan.

A west-wing conversion in Milwaukee got a line no other prospect could receive:

Converting Hilton Milwaukee’s west wing into the independent Marc Hotel means separating network, cabling and carrier requirements inside a building that will still serve two hotel identities.

The rubric, and the fix it needed

Each line was scored 0 to 2 on four criteria: specific, accurate about the prospect, relevant to JET, and sounds human. The version labels were shuffled before scoring.

The first scoring pass marked almost every version 3 line as "unsupported," including general points like carrier circuits needing to be ordered early. The rubric hadn't separated facts about the prospect from JET's own expertise, so I rewrote the accuracy criterion to cover prospect facts only and judged domain points under relevance. That pass also gave nearly identical totals across rows, which is a sign of a lazy scorer, so the rerun told it to judge each row on its own. The scores above come from the corrected rubric. A prompt test is only as good as its scoring, so the rubric gets versioned and logged like the prompts.

Limits, and what I'd test next

The same model family wrote and scored the lines, which risks it favoring its own style. Next time I'd have a person spot-check ten rows, or use a different model as the scorer.

Two signals had already passed their opening dates, but the research prompt still read them as under construction. Version 4 would pass today's date in and set the stage from the dates.

Ten of the 25 source articles blocked the fetch, so those rows relied on the one-sentence signal. In Clay, a second scrape provider or a search-based enrichment would fill that gap.

This measures copy quality before sending. Reply rates are the real test, so the next step is a live split between the winning prompt and the current one.

How I'd run this at BuyerSight

Every new client segment would get the same loop before its first campaign: two or three prompt versions on 25 real prospects, blind scoring against a rubric written for that client's offer, and a one-page knowledge base entry on what changed and why. The winning prompt becomes the default for that client, and live reply data decides the next version.

Over time, the knowledge base becomes a record of which kinds of detail earn replies in which markets. New clients start from that record instead of a blank prompt.
