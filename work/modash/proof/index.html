The read

What a larger buyer sees today

Avery Schrader wrote in the VP of Marketing posting that people still mostly know Modash for search. I looked at the site the way a marketing lead at a brand with 300 creators would. The product story is strong. The larger-buyer story is missing.

There is no enterprise pageTyping modash.io/enterprise returns a 404 page. It is the first URL many larger buyers try.
The Enterprise plan is described by volumeThe pricing page describes Enterprise as "Campaigns above 250 creators", from $14,700 a year, with higher limits, payout tiers, a custom tracking domain and a success manager.
The homepage speaks to Shopify storesThe homepage headline is "Manage and grow your influencer program", and the subhead opens "Shopify-first influencer marketing platform". A third-party roundup on Influencer Marketing Hub repeats that framing, calling Modash best for "in-house marketers at eCommerce brands using Shopify".
Proof is short quotesThe customers page holds about 13 short quotes and no case studies with results. Only two quotes carry a number.
The review count is smallG2's comparison pages for GRIN and CreatorIQ show Modash rated about 4.9 from about 18 reviews. GRIN has 483 reviews and CreatorIQ has 568. Larger buyers read those shortlists.

The product itself has moved well past search. Modash has standalone pages for creator payments and affiliate programs, and it bought the relationship tool Promoty. Those pages are good. They sit as separate pages, though, and nothing ties them into one reason a larger team should run its whole program in Modash.

Who the larger buyer is

The Essentials plan is built for campaigns of up to 100 creators with two seats. The buyer the next stage needs runs several hundred creators across markets and has finance and legal in the room.

That buyer already believes in creators. Their pain sits after the search: paying hundreds of people in different countries, keeping contracts and usage rights straight, and showing a CFO which creators drove sales. Avery named these problems himself on his LinkedIn profile: strategy, operations and measurement are hard, and "the tooling sucks."

The positioning

Modash runs your whole creator program in one place, from the first search to the last payout, with sales tracked back to each creator.

Search stays the way in, because it is what Modash is known for. Payments and measurement become the reason a larger team commits, because those are the jobs that break first at scale. Every product Modash already sells maps to one step of the program.

The cross-sell path

The pricing page already sets the path. Essentials covers search, management, tracking and gifting. Payments and Affiliate start on the $499 Performance plan. Enterprise adds scale and a success manager.

What I can't see from outside is when customers get pushed along that path. I would test triggers like these, each one a hypothesis until Modash's usage data confirms it.

  • A brand reaches its tracked-creator limit on Essentials. It sees a prompt to move to Performance, with payments shown as the next step.
  • A brand sends gifting to more than 50 creators in a month. It gets an email on running those creators as affiliates.
  • A brand adds a second market or a third seat. Sales gets a handover with the account's usage history.
  • A Performance account passes the $10,000 shared fee-free payout limit. It is offered Enterprise payout tiers.

What I'd measure

  • Expansion rate from Essentials to Performance and Enterprise, by trigger.
  • Share of new accounts using more than one product within 90 days.
  • Enterprise pipeline from the new page, and the win rate on those deals.
  • Net revenue retention by plan and cohort.
  • G2 review count each month against GRIN and CreatorIQ.
