A lifecycle and monetization plan for Smart Timetable

Your messaging is already clear. The channel that would use it does not exist.

James Praise · Marketing and GTM Operator

Testing the in-app onboarding and paywall requires installing and using the app, which I do not do on a company's behalf. Everything below comes from public surfaces: your own marketing site and FAQ, your App Store listing, and your public review history. I have flagged what that leaves unchecked.

1. Your gap is not the one I expected

I should say this first, because it changes the whole plan. I have written two of these recently for other apps, and in both the problem was the same: an unclear line between free and paid that users discovered mid-task, with public complaints to prove it.

You do not have that problem. Your FAQ lays out free against premium in plain, specific language, and there is no confusion-driven complaint pattern in your reviews to point at. So the rest of this is not that plan with your name swapped in.

Your own FAQ. Free covers daily and weekly schedules, the Today widget, Apple Watch support, and sending a schedule to a friend. Premium adds multiple schedules, reminders, cross-device sync, live-sync sharing, and file attachments. Most freemium apps never write this out this plainly in public.

2. The premium moment already works, once someone reaches it

4.6 stars across 3,400-plus US ratings, version 11.0.6 updated four days before I looked. Your own site reports 7.9 million downloads and 75,400-plus positive reviews.

And conversion is fast when someone finds what they need. One reviewer put it directly:

"I tried Smart Timetable and it was so much more helpful than expected and I paid premium almost immediately."

She needed exactly one premium capability, complex rotating A/B schedules with copy and preset tools, and paid the moment she found it. Another reviewer, using it for homeschooling and work, "bought it waiting for all wonderful updates." That is a loyal paying customer, not a reluctant one.

So the conversion mechanism is not broken. Nothing is nudging anyone toward it.

3. There is no lifecycle layer at all

Nothing on smart-timetable.app, and as far as the public listing shows nothing in the app's onboarding either, captures an email address or moves a free user toward the specific premium features that convert fast once discovered. There is an "Install the app" call to action and then nothing between that and the app stores. No list, no welcome sequence, no reason to expect the next touch after install to come from you at all.

That matters more here than it would for most apps, because of how seasonal this product is.

A student sets up a schedule in September. The app is essential for a semester. Then a break arrives, and nothing brings them back when the next term starts and they need a new schedule.

That is the single highest-leverage moment this product has. It is predictable, it is calendar-driven, it recurs every year, and nothing is built to use it. It is also the one thing a purely in-app product with no email capture structurally cannot do for itself.

4. What I would build, in order

Email capture, first

There is no list today, so writing sequences is premature. A simple in-app or post-install prompt, a schedule reminder saved by email or an emailed copy of a schedule, would be enough to start one. This is the gate on everything else.

A welcome sequence that orients rather than sells

Help someone actually finish setting up their first schedule. The premium features that convert fast, rotating schedules, sync, file attachments, only become obviously necessary once a schedule is genuinely complex or shared. Selling before that moment is selling to someone who does not need it yet.

A triggered email at the moment of real premium need

When usage suggests it: multiple schedules created, or a schedule shared with someone else. Framed around what they are already doing instead of a generic upgrade pitch.

The semester-start reactivation campaign

Timed to the academic calendar, reaching anyone who set up a schedule last term and has not opened the app since. This is the piece I would build for, and the other three exist partly to make it possible.

5. One smaller thing, flagged as one data point

Your reviews arrive organically in Spanish, French, Portuguese, German, Chinese, Russian, Korean, Turkish and Italian, with no paid localisation behind that reach. One French-speaking reviewer wrote, translated: "even without paying for the paid version, too expensive for me, the app remains very functional."

That is one reviewer, not a trend, and I am not going to present it as one. It is worth investigating whether a single global price point is leaving international conversion on the table the way it would for any product with this much organic non-US demand. Your own numbers would answer that in an afternoon. Mine cannot.

6. How you would know, including how to prove me wrong

There is no baseline today, so the first real number is the email capture rate itself, starting from zero.

Then: free-to-premium conversion for users who received a lifecycle touch against users who converted purely through in-app discovery. That tells you directly whether the lifecycle layer is adding conversions or just reaching people who would have converted anyway, which is the honest question.

And specifically for the semester-start campaign, which is the claim I am most confident about and therefore the one most worth testing properly: return rate among lapsed users contacted at the start of a new term, against a holdout group that is not contacted. If the holdout comes back at the same rate, the campaign is not doing the work and I am wrong. Better to find that out on one term than to scale it on a hunch.

7. What I could not check

Testing the in-app onboarding and paywall requires installing and using the app, which I do not do on a company's behalf. Everything above comes from your FAQ, your marketing site and your public App Store review history.

The consequence worth naming: if some in-app email capture already exists that is not visible from outside, then the first item in my build order is already done and the plan starts one step further along. That is worth confirming before anyone builds anything.

8. What not to touch

Your personal, detailed responses to nearly every review, often referencing the reviewer's specific situation and folding their suggestions into a visible roadmap, are a real asset and a large part of why loyalty here is strong. The FAQ's clarity on free against premium is the other one, and it is the reason there is no confusion-driven complaint pattern in the first place.

Neither needs fixing. The opportunity is entirely in building a layer that does not exist yet, on top of a foundation that is already solid.
