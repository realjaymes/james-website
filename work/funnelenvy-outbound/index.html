Case Study — FunnelEnvy Client Outbound

Cold outbound for five client accounts at once, about 9,000 emails each a month

FunnelEnvy is a growth agency for B2B software companies. I ran cold email outbound for five of its client accounts at the same time. I built one system to find buyers and check their work emails, and it researched each company, wrote the emails and sent every reply to the right person. On average, each client got 10 meetings and $151,962 in new pipeline, the value of new sales opportunities, a month.

What I Ran

Each client reached 1,000 companies a month, three people at each company, with a sequence of three emails. That comes to about 9,000 emails a month for each client, and up to 45,000 a month across the five. The client names stay private.

Each client had its own workspace holding its offer, positioning and brand, what earlier campaigns had taught us, meeting notes and that week's priorities. Every step of the system reads from that workspace. That kept five different sets of buyers apart while one system wrote to all of them. Smartlead, the cold email sending tool, sent the emails. Contacts and deals went into each client's own CRM (customer relationship management system).

Apollo, a sales contact database, finds buyers by job title, and Outscraper pulls local businesses from Google Maps. Clay, or a Python script I wrote, then asks one email data provider after another until one returns a work email. AI agents, research scripts built on a language model, read each company's website, job posts and public registers. They look for a recent reason to write, which I call a signal, and for the facts each email needs.

Three details pick what each email offers: the signal, the problem the research found at that company, and the person's job title. Variables, conditional lines and spintax turn that into the email itself. Spintax is a set of alternative wordings the sender picks between, so emails to similar people don't read word for word the same.

Smartlead sends up to 30 emails a day from each inbox. Every inbox is warmed first, so it builds a normal sending history before any cold email goes out. Tracking stays turned off, because it adds an invisible image and rewritten links to every email, and spam filters treat both as warning signs.

Three Checks Before Anything Sends

An address counts only when two separate email checkers both pass it. Some company mail servers accept every address, real or not. These are called catch-all domains, and one check can't tell a real inbox on them from a made-up one. I test each domain with an address I invented, and if the server accepts it, I mark that domain as a risk. Anyone already in the client's CRM comes off the list too, so nobody gets a cold email from a company they already talk to.

Nothing sends until I've seen every email filled in. My preview tool shows each email for each lead and flags broken fields, such as a missing first name or company name. It exports the checked file, and only that file goes into Smartlead.

I also check every reply marked interested, because sending tools often label a reply interested when it isn't.

Every Reply Gets Routed

n8n, an automation tool, reads each reply and sends it down one of five routes. The sender in each route is the person at the client whose name is on the emails.

  • Interested: a deal opens in the client's CRM, and Slack tells the sender to reply within the hour.
  • Gives a date, such as "try us after our renewal in March": the emails stop, and the CRM reminds the sender to write again 60 days before that date.
  • Wrong person: the company goes back to the first step to find the right buyer.
  • Away, and names a colleague: we find and check the colleague's email and send them a short email that says who pointed us to them.
  • Unsubscribes: the address is blocked in Smartlead and the CRM the same day.

Leads who never reply wait on a watch list until a new signal gives a fresh reason to write. Slack also tells the team when a campaign pauses itself or finishes.

Results

These are the monthly averages for one client, across the five accounts.

  • 9,000 emails to 3,000 people at 1,000 companies
  • 102 replies, from 3.4% of the people emailed
  • 20 companies said yes to a conversation, and 10 meetings were held
  • $151,962 in new pipeline
  • $27,353 in closed revenue, 11.6 times what the campaign cost to run

Each week's report splits the results by tier, signal and email version. A tier is how valuable the account is to the client. That split shows which reasons to write, and which versions of the first email, lead to meetings, and the next month's lists and emails change to match. Testing new angles, new audiences and campaigns aimed at named accounts against reply data lifted reply rates 27%.

How I Fix a Campaign

I review a struggling campaign across twelve areas. They include who to target, why now, the offer, the message, the sending inboxes, how replies are handled and how results are measured. The numbers usually show which area broke, and I change that area first.

For example, if bounces stay low and replies come in but almost none are interested, the emails are reaching real people. I would test a new offer or message before I touched the lists or the inboxes.
