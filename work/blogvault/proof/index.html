A lifecycle and monetization plan for BlogVault

A decade of your own one-star reviews are all saying the same thing.

James Praise · Marketing and GTM Operator

BlogVault's trial requires creating an account with an email, password and site URL, which I do not do on a company's behalf. Everything below comes from public surfaces only: your WordPress.org listing and its FAQ, your pricing page, and twelve years of your own public review history. I have flagged the gaps this leaves.

1. The install-time trust gap, in your own reviews

Your WordPress.org listing shows the plugin as free to install with no price anywhere near the download button. That is standard for the directory. BlogVault, though, has no free tier at all. Your own FAQ says it plainly: "BlogVault is a paid backup service." What the listing implies and what actually happens are two different things.

Your WordPress.org listing. The download button carries no price, and nothing above the fold says the plugin converts to a paid annual plan after seven days.

That gap has produced the same complaint, in different words, across your entire twelve-year review history.

A sample of your one-star reviews: "It isn't free!", "No free version", "Bait & Switch", "Paid Plugin. WordPress Should Say That", "They don't mention you have to pay", "It's a PAY to use plugin."

I read the titles of all 32 of your one-star reviews and opened several in full. The pattern runs from 2014 to 2023 and accounts for a meaningful share of them. This is not one bad week or one angry customer. It is a decade of the same misunderstanding, and every one of those reviews is public, permanent, and sitting next to your 4.6-star average for anyone deciding whether to install.

Your WordPress.org FAQ does answer this. The question is there, "Do you have a free plan? How does it work?", and the answer is honest. It lives one click down in an accordion, though, after someone has already formed an impression from a listing that shows no price. You are not hiding the trial model. The listing simply says something different from the FAQ, and most people never reach the FAQ.

2. The moment someone decides to pay you

Your trial model has a real advantage over a feature-gated freemium plugin. Nothing is locked. The trial is fully featured, so nobody hits a silent wall mid-task. The gap sits at the other end, when someone has decided to convert.

Your FAQ answer to "How do I upgrade from my free trial to a premium version of the account?" is "please take the help of this guide." That is a link out, not an answer.

At the exact moment someone wants to give you money, you send them to look for instructions. Not the three plans. Not a checkout button. A guide.

3. What already works, and should not be touched

Your pricing page: three plans, transparent per-site pricing, a POPULAR badge steering people to Business, a money-back guarantee with a named window, and real customer testimonials.

Your pricing page is genuinely clear, and it is worth naming because the fix below builds on it instead of replacing it. Three plans, transparent per-site pricing, a POPULAR badge doing the steering work, a 100% money-back guarantee with a specific 14-day window, and real named testimonials. None of that needs changing.

The problem is entirely upstream, at the listing-to-trial moment, and at the one downstream moment that matters most. It is not in the pricing structure.

4. What I would change

On the WordPress.org listing

Add one honest line near the top of the description. Something close to: "BlogVault includes a 7-day free trial with full access, no credit card required. Paid plans start at $99/year after your trial." One sentence removes the ambiguity that has generated a decade of one-star reviews, and it does it before anyone installs instead of after.

On the FAQ answer that currently deflects

Replace "please take the help of this guide" with the three plans and a direct link to checkout, and put the same thing inside the WordPress admin dashboard rather than only on the FAQ page.

Inside the trial, days five to seven

A visible, persistent reminder of how many trial days remain and what happens when it ends. Whether backups stop, whether the site stays accessible read-only, whether data is deleted, whichever is actually true. With one click to the right plan. I could not confirm from outside an account whether this exists today, so it is worth checking before building it.

The email sequence the deadline makes possible

A seven-day trial is a tight, dated window, which makes email more powerful here than in a permanent-freemium model where there is no natural deadline. A welcome email at signup confirming the trial is fully featured and naming the exact expiry date. A mid-trial email around day four showing what has already been backed up, which makes the value concrete instead of abstract. A day-six email built on the specific expiry date with a direct upgrade link. And a short win-back for anyone who lets the trial lapse, reminding them their backup history is still there and one plan away from resuming.

5. How you would know if any of it worked

Four numbers, none of which need a heavy analytics build to start:

  • Trial signup rate from the WordPress.org listing, watched in both directions, because the added disclosure line could reduce installs while improving the people who do install.
  • Trial-to-paid conversion by day of trial, which shows exactly when people convert and when they drop.
  • Click-through from the FAQ upgrade answer to checkout.
  • The rate of new one-star reviews mentioning price, which is a public leading indicator that the listing fix is landing.

A conversion-rate view segmented by trial cohort would surface whether any of this moves within a few trial cycles.

6. What I could not check

Your trial requires creating an account with an email, a password and a site URL. I do not create accounts on a company's behalf without them present, so I did not use the product. Everything above comes from public surfaces only, and I have said so wherever it matters.

That leaves real gaps. I do not know what your in-trial experience currently says about expiry. I do not know your actual trial-to-paid rate, and I do not know your real blended revenue per customer, so I have deliberately not built a revenue projection on numbers I would be guessing at. You have all three. At 80,000-plus installs, even a small shift in trial-to-paid conversion is meaningful revenue without a single new install, but the size of it is yours to calculate, not mine to estimate.

One thing I did confirm and correct along the way: your checkout prices are currently 50% below list, so the real blended figure is a lot closer to $100 a year than the $250 I first assumed. That changed my own arithmetic downward, which is worth saying out loud.
