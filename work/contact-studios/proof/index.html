Why I wrote this

Your Meta Muse post gives a quick check: if you can't highlight the text on a page, an AI agent can't read it, and pricing loaded by a script is one of the first places to look. The draft

Can AI agents read your pricing page? We checked nine

Meta's new AI agent, Muse, recommended one of our clients over its biggest competitor. The buyer never searched for our client's name. They asked Muse for the best product in the category, and Muse opened the websites, read the product pages and compared them on the buyer's behalf.

That changes what a pricing page is for. A buyer used to read it. Now an agent reads it first, and the buyer reads the agent's summary.

So we ran a test. On 17 November 2026 we fetched the pricing pages of nine creator platforms the way an AI agent does, and checked what each one could actually read.

What an agent sees when it opens your page

When you open a pricing page, your browser downloads the page and then runs its JavaScript, which often fills in the plans and prices. Most AI agents skip the second step.

Vercel tracked the crawlers from OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, ByteDance and Perplexity and found that none of them run JavaScript. They read the raw HTML the server sends back. Anything a script adds later never reaches them.

The highlight test is the fastest way to spot trouble. If you can't highlight a price, it's probably an image, and no agent can read it. But highlighting has one blind spot: text a script drew on the page still highlights. To see what an agent sees, you have to look at the raw HTML.

The test

We picked nine platforms that creators compare when they choose where to sell: beehiiv, Kajabi, Shopify, Kit, Teachable, Podia, Circle, Gumroad and Thinkific.

For each pricing page we made one request with the same user agent ChatGPT uses when it browses for a user, ran no JavaScript, and stripped out the code. Then we counted what was left: the words a reader would see, and the dollar amounts among them.

We also checked each page for structured data, the schema.org markup that lists a product and its price in a format machines read without guessing.

Result 1: every page passed

All nine pricing pages ship their prices in the raw HTML. An agent that opens any of them can read the plans and what they cost.

Gumroad's single dollar amount is not a gap. It charges a fee per sale rather than a plan price, and that fee is in the HTML.

For a creator tool, that sets the bar. If your pricing page loads its prices with a script, you're behind every platform on this list. An agent comparing you with them will find their prices and miss yours.

Result 2: five of nine also hand agents a clean copy

beehiiv, Kajabi, Teachable, Podia and Circle add their prices to structured data as well. Shopify, Kit, Gumroad and Thinkific don't.

The prices are readable either way. Structured data removes the guesswork. A pricing page holds monthly prices, annual prices, add-ons and per-sale fees side by side, and the markup states which number belongs to which plan. Four of the nine (beehiiv, Kajabi, Podia and Circle) also mark up their pricing questions and answers.

Passing the highlight test gets you into the comparison. Clean structured data makes it harder for an agent to misquote you once you're there.

Check your own page in ten minutes

  1. Highlight every price and every customer quote. Anything you can't select is an image, and the fix is to put that text on the page as text.
  2. Open the page source (Cmd+Option+U on a Mac, Ctrl+U on Windows) and search it for your cheapest price. If it isn't there, a script adds it, and agents never see it.
  3. Do the same for one testimonial. Pick a sentence from a quote on the page and search the source for it.
  4. Run the page through the Schema Markup Validator and see whether your plans and prices appear as offers.
  5. Ask ChatGPT what your product costs, then compare its answer with your pricing page.

What this test doesn't tell you

We fetched each page once, on one day, with one user agent. A page can serve different HTML to different visitors, and agents change how they fetch. Reading a price is also only the first step. Which product an agent recommends depends on everything else it reads, which is why we'd run step 5 every month.

Appendix: how the check was run

  • Date: 17 November 2026. One request per page, with the user agent Mozilla/5.0 (compatible; ChatGPT-User/1.0; +https://openai.com/bot).
  • Visible text: the raw HTML with script, style, noscript, template and svg blocks removed, then tags removed. Words are counted as space-separated tokens.
  • Dollar amounts: distinct matches of a dollar sign followed by a number in the visible text.
  • Structured data: application/ld+json blocks searched for a price or lowPrice field, and for the FAQPage type.
  • Skool was part of the first set and was dropped, because its site was down for maintenance during the run.
  • The check is a short Python script that uses only the standard library. I'm happy to share it.
